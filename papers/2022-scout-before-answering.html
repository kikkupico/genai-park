Before this

Large language models were weak at problems that need several steps, such as arithmetic word problems, even when they could do each step on its own. Asked for an answer, a walker had to put the answer down as its very next footprint, with only a single step's worth of computation to find it.

Large language models performed poorly on multi-step problems such as arithmetic word problems, even when they could perform each step. Prompted for a direct answer, the model must produce it as the next token, using a single forward pass of computation.

Picks up what was left open in 2022 · Judge where walks endReinforcement learning from human feedback.

In the walk

The walker can't see ahead, so it walks ahead on scratch paper: footprints that work the problem out before the answer. Once the answer is written, those footprints stop being the product. Scouting means walks taken only to choose the real one. Tree of Thoughts scouts several branches and drops the bad ones. Reasoning models are trained, by judging only the final answer, to scout long and well.

In the mechanism

Prompting, and later training, a model to write intermediate reasoning before its answer improves accuracy on multi-step problems. Tree of Thoughts runs a search over partial reasoning. DeepSeek-R1 trained long reasoning with reinforcement learning, rewarding only checkable final answers.

A closer look: why writing the working helpswhy intermediate tokens help

The 2022 paper's method was just a different road. Instead of examples showing question then answer, it showed question, working, answer:

Q: Roger has 5 tennis balls. He buys 2 more cans of tennis balls. Each can has 3 tennis balls. How many tennis balls does he have now?
A: Roger started with 5 balls. 2 cans of 3 tennis balls each is 6 tennis balls. 5 + 6 = 11. The answer is 11.

Shown eight such examples, a large model wrote its own working on new problems, and accuracy on grade-school math rose sharply. In walker terms, every footprint gets the same fixed amount of thought. Writing out the working gives the walker more footprints to think with, and somewhere to put partial results, since the road is its only memory.

Branching. Tree of Thoughts makes the scouting explicit: write several candidate next steps, have the model rate each, keep the promising ones, abandon the rest. On the Game of 24, a puzzle that makes 24 from four numbers, GPT-4 solved 4% of problems with plain chain-of-thought and 74% with this search.

Training for it. Reasoning models, starting with OpenAI's o1 in 2024, are trained to scout. DeepSeek-R1 described one way openly: reinforcement learning that rewards only a correct, checkable final answer, on math and code. Nobody graded the working. Over training the scouting walks grew longer on their own, and the model began to check and revise its own steps.

Chain-of-thought prompting changes only the few-shot exemplars: instead of question and answer, each shows question, reasoning and answer:

Q: Roger has 5 tennis balls. He buys 2 more cans of tennis balls. Each can has 3 tennis balls. How many tennis balls does he have now?
A: Roger started with 5 balls. 2 cans of 3 tennis balls each is 6 tennis balls. 5 + 6 = 11. The answer is 11.

With eight such exemplars, large models generated their own reasoning on new problems, and accuracy on GSM8K grade-school math rose sharply. Each generated token gets a fixed amount of computation, so intermediate tokens add serial computation, and they store partial results in the context, which is the model's only working memory.

Search. Tree of Thoughts generates multiple candidate reasoning steps, has the model evaluate them, and explores the tree with breadth- or depth-first search, backtracking from poor branches. On the Game of 24, GPT-4 solved 4% of problems with chain-of-thought prompting and 74% with Tree of Thoughts.

Training. Reasoning models, starting with OpenAI's o1 (2024), are trained to produce long reasoning. DeepSeek-R1 published one method: reinforcement learning with rule-based rewards for correct, verifiable final answers in math and code, with no supervision of the reasoning itself. Over training, response lengths grew, and behaviors such as self-verification and revision emerged.

What it changed

It opened a second thing to scale. Besides training a bigger walker, you can let a walker scout longer at the moment of answering, and harder problems repay more scouting. It also brings back a judge that, unlike the 2022 human preference judge, can't be flattered: the answer is either right or not.

Scouting is still walking. A wrong turn can't be erased, only followed by footprints saying it was wrong. And scouting can't find a fact the walker never saw.

It opened a second scaling axis: test-time compute. Besides scaling training, performance can be improved by allowing more reasoning tokens at inference, with harder problems benefiting more. Verifiable rewards also provide a training signal that is much harder to exploit than a learned reward model.

Reasoning is still autoregressive generation. A mistake can't be deleted, only followed by tokens that correct it, and reasoning can't supply facts absent from the training data.

Left open

A walker can't take back a footprint: "backing up" means walking on down a road that says "that was wrong". And no amount of scouting finds a fact the walker never saw.Generated tokens can't be retracted, only corrected by later tokens. And reasoning can't recover facts the model never learned. → 2023
