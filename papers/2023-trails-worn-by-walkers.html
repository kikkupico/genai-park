The Curse of Recursion: Training on Generated Data Makes Models Forget (2023)

Before this

Language models are trained on text scraped from the web. As generated text spreads across the web, later models will be trained partly on earlier models' output, whether or not anyone intends it. This paper asked what happens when that loop runs for several generations.

Language models are trained on web-scale scraped text. As generated content spreads online, future training sets will contain the outputs of earlier models, whether or not anyone intends it. This paper studied what happens when models are trained recursively on generated data.

Formally: generation \(n+1\) is fit to samples from generation \(n\), \(\theta_{n+1} = \arg\max_\theta \sum_i \log p_\theta(x_i)\) with \(x_i\sim p_{\theta_n}\). What does \(p_{\theta_n}\) converge to?

In the walk

Train each generation of walkers on footprints left by the one before, and the trails narrow. The common way gets wider; rare ways fade until nobody walks them. Self-play game players escape this, because a win or a loss keeps correcting their trails. Here nothing does.

In the mechanism

Training repeatedly on model-generated data loses the tails of the original distribution first, then drifts toward a narrow version of it. The authors call this model collapse. Keeping some original human data in the mix slows it down.

\[x^{(n)}_{1:M}\sim p_{\theta_n},\qquad \theta_{n+1} = \arg\max_\theta \sum_{i=1}^{M}\log p_\theta\big(x^{(n)}_i\big)\]

With finite \(M\), events of small probability are missed and get less mass at the next step. Repeated, the tails vanish first and the distribution narrows. The authors call this model collapse. Mixing in original human data slows it down.

A closer look: how trails narrowwhy recursive training narrows the distribution

Start with the simplest case. Take a bell curve, draw a thousand samples, and fit a new bell curve to them. Draw a thousand from the new curve, fit again, and repeat. Each sample under-represents the rare far ends, a little at random, and each fit inherits those gaps. Over many rounds the spread shrinks, and the tails vanish first.

The paper finds two sources, both present in real models:

  • Sampling. A finite set of walks misses rare routes. Once a route is missed, the next generation's trails don't have it.
  • Imperfect fit. Every model approximates its training trails slightly wrong, and the errors add up generation after generation.

With language models fine-tuned repeatedly on their own text, the same pattern appeared: rare continuations disappeared, and later generations drifted toward repetitive, strange text. One example in the paper begins with medieval church architecture and, nine generations later, has become a list of jackrabbits with different-colored tails.

Keeping some of the original human data in each round slowed the collapse. Later work found that adding generated data on top of the original, rather than replacing it, avoids much of the damage.

The simplest case: fit a Gaussian to \(M\) samples, draw \(M\) samples from the fit, refit, and repeat. With the unbiased variance estimate, each refit multiplies the variance by an independent factor \(\chi^2_{M-1}/(M-1)\):

\[\sigma_n^2 = \sigma_0^2 \prod_{k=1}^{n} \frac{\chi^2_{M-1,\,k}}{M-1}\]

Each factor has mean 1, so \(\mathbb{E}[\sigma_n^2] = \sigma_0^2\). But by Jensen's inequality each factor has negative expected logarithm, so \(\log\sigma_n^2\) is a random walk with negative drift, and \(\sigma_n^2 \to 0\) almost surely. Each finite sample under-represents the tails, each refit inherits the error, and the tails disappear first.

The paper identifies two compounding error sources:

  • Statistical approximation error. Finite samples miss low-probability events, so the next model assigns them less probability, or none.
  • Functional approximation error. Each model's function class and training are imperfect, and errors accumulate across generations.

Fine-tuning language models repeatedly on their own outputs showed the same pattern: low-probability content vanished and later generations produced degenerate, repetitive text. One example in the paper begins with medieval church architecture and, nine generations later, has turned into a list of jackrabbits with different-colored tails.

Retaining a fraction of the original human data in each generation slowed the degradation. Later work found that accumulating synthetic data alongside the original, rather than replacing it, largely avoids collapse.

What it changed

The paper, later published in Nature (2024), made data provenance part of the conversation: human-written text, and records of what is human, became more valuable.

The contrast with the 2022 reasoning entry is the lesson. Training a walker on its own walks is fine when a judge keeps checking which walks were right, as with checkable math answers or self-play games won or lost. Collapse is what happens when there is no judge.

The paper, later published in Nature (2024), made data provenance a concern: verified human-generated data, and the ability to tell it apart from generated data, gained value.

It contrasts with RL on verifiable rewards and with self-play: training on self-generated data works when an external signal (correct answers, game outcomes) filters or weights it. Collapse is what happens without such a signal.
