Before this

By 2019 bigger language models were clearly better, but nobody could say by how much, or how to spend a fixed budget: a bigger model, more data, or longer training? Architecture choices such as depth, width and attention heads were tuned by trial. This paper measured all of these across hundreds of training runs to see which actually mattered.

By 2019 it was clear that larger language models performed better, but not how much better, or how to allocate a fixed compute budget between model size, data and training steps. Architectural hyperparameters such as depth, width and number of heads were tuned empirically. This paper measured these relationships systematically over many training runs.

Formally: how does test loss \(L\) depend on parameters \(N\), data \(D\) and compute \(C \approx 6ND\), and for a fixed \(C\), which \(N\) and \(D\) minimize it?

In the walk

Send more walkers through the park and draw the ground more finely, and the blaze comes closer to how people really walk. That much was expected. The surprise was how smoothly it happens: every tenfold increase cuts the error by about the same fraction, so on a log-log chart the points fall on a straight line. A straight line can be extended, so the next walker could be forecast before anyone built it. Scale became something to buy on purpose.

In the mechanism

Test loss falls as a power law in parameters, training tokens and compute, across more than seven orders of magnitude. Details of shape, such as depth versus width, matter far less than size. The 2020 paper concluded that for a fixed budget, the model should grow faster than the data. The 2022 paper (Chinchilla) corrected this: grow both together, about 20 training tokens per parameter.

\[L(N) \approx \Big(\frac{N_c}{N}\Big)^{\alpha_N},\qquad \alpha_N \approx 0.076\]

A power law is a straight line in log–log coordinates, so a fit on small runs predicts the loss of much larger ones. Similar laws hold in data \(D\) and compute \(C\).

A closer look: reading the straight linepower laws and compute-optimal allocation

On a log-log chart, the test loss falls on a straight line in three quantities: parameters N, training tokens D and compute C, as long as the other two aren't holding it back. A straight line on log-log axes is a power law:

L(N) ≈ (Nc / N)0.076

The exponent is small, which is the point. Doubling the model cuts the loss by only about 5%; ten times the model cuts it by about 16%. No single step is dramatic, but the step is the same size every time, across more than seven orders of magnitude. So you can train a ladder of small models, fit the line, and read off what a model a hundred times larger will score before spending the money.

The same runs showed that shape barely matters within a wide range: a model's depth versus width moved the loss far less than its total size did.

The correction. The 2020 paper advised spending most of a larger budget on parameters and comparatively little on data. In 2022 the Chinchilla paper redid the measurement, adjusting the learning-rate schedule for each run length, and found parameters and data should grow together: about 20 tokens per parameter. Its 70-billion-parameter model, trained on 1.4 trillion tokens, beat a 280-billion-parameter model trained with the same compute on far fewer tokens. Many models of the time had been too big for their data.

Cross-entropy test loss follows a power law in each of non-embedding parameters \(N\), dataset size \(D\) in tokens and compute \(C\), when not bottlenecked by the other two:

\[L(N) = \Big(\frac{N_c}{N}\Big)^{\alpha_N},\qquad L(D) = \Big(\frac{D_c}{D}\Big)^{\alpha_D},\qquad L(C_{\min}) = \Big(\frac{C_c}{C_{\min}}\Big)^{\alpha_C}\]

with \(\alpha_N \approx 0.076\), \(\alpha_D \approx 0.095\) and \(\alpha_C \approx 0.050\). Taking logs, \(\log L = \alpha_N(\log N_c - \log N)\): a line of slope \(-\alpha_N\). A small exponent means slow but steady returns: doubling \(N\) multiplies \(L\) by \(2^{-0.076}\approx 0.95\), about 5% lower, and \(10\times N\) by \(10^{-0.076}\approx 0.84\). The trend held across more than seven orders of magnitude.

Within a broad range, architectural shape (depth versus width, number of heads) had a much smaller effect than \(N\).

Chinchilla (2022). With \(C \approx 6ND\), the compute-optimal model is \(N_{\text{opt}} \propto C^{a}\), \(D_{\text{opt}} \propto C^{b}\). The 2020 paper found \(N_{\text{opt}} \propto C^{0.73}\): grow the model much faster than the data. Hoffmann et al. repeated the analysis with learning-rate schedules matched to each training length and found \(a \approx b \approx 0.5\), about 20 tokens per parameter. Their 70B-parameter model trained on 1.4T tokens outperformed the 280B-parameter Gopher, trained with the same compute on far fewer tokens. Many existing large models were undertrained.

What it changed

Scale became something to plan. Large training runs are now designed by fitting small runs and extrapolating, and frontier labs report predicting a model's final loss from runs using a tiny fraction of its compute.

What the line predicts is narrow: how closely the walker's footprints match human text. Particular abilities don't always follow it smoothly, and a lower loss doesn't make a walker more truthful or more helpful. That needed a different kind of trail.
