Before this

Speech recognition shows the cut-path problem at its sharpest. Rules written by experts about how sounds join into words kept breaking on real voices, accents and noise. From the 1970s, groups at IBM and Carnegie Mellon and elsewhere turned to statistics instead: describe speech as a probabilistic process and learn its numbers from recordings. A line often attributed to IBM's Fred Jelinek sums up the mood: every time he fired a linguist, the recognizer got better. Rabiner's tutorial gathered the method into the form everyone learned it from.

In the walk

The two kinds of path meet. Designers still lay out the garden, deciding which stations exist and which paths join them. But they no longer decide how likely each path is. Walkers wear that in: here, thousands of recorded voices saying "speech".

In the mechanism

A hidden Markov model is a generative model: it describes how a sequence of sounds is produced by walking through hidden states. Designers chose the states and allowed transitions; the probabilities were learned from data with the Baum–Welch algorithm. This hand-laid structure with learned numbers was how statistical AI worked until deep learning, around 2012.

A closer look: the garden for one worda left-to-right HMM for one word

Picture the garden for the word "speech". A designer cuts a row of stations, roughly one per sound: s, p, ee, ch. Each station has two cut paths: one that loops back to itself, because a sound can last a short or long time, and one forward to the next station. There is no path backward and none that skips ahead. That layout is the hand-laid part.

Two sets of numbers are worn in from recordings: how often walkers loop rather than move on at each station, and what each station tends to sound like, as a probability over short slices of audio. The stations are hidden: a recording reveals only sounds, never which station produced each one.

Rabiner framed everything as three questions:

  • How likely is this recording, given this garden? Add up over every possible walk through the stations (the forward algorithm). Recognition asks this of each word's garden and picks the most likely word.
  • Which walk most likely produced it? Find the single best path, efficiently (the Viterbi algorithm). That lines up each slice of audio with a sound.
  • How should the trails be worn? Guess the walks, re-count, repeat until the numbers settle (Baum–Welch, a form of expectation–maximization).

A word model such as "speech" is a chain of hidden states, roughly one per phone: s, p, ee, ch. Each state has a self-transition, so a phone can last a variable number of frames, and a transition to the next state. Backward transitions and skips are disallowed. This topology is chosen by the designer.

Two sets of parameters are estimated from data: transition probabilities, and per-state emission distributions over acoustic feature vectors (in practice, Gaussian mixtures over MFCC frames). The states are latent: the observations are frames, and the state sequence that produced them is unobserved.

Rabiner organizes the method around three problems:

  • Evaluation. Compute P(observations | model) by summing over all state sequences, with dynamic programming (the forward algorithm). Isolated-word recognition picks the word model with the highest likelihood.
  • Decoding. Find the single most likely state sequence (the Viterbi algorithm), which aligns frames to phones.
  • Learning. Estimate the parameters that maximize the likelihood of the training data with Baum–Welch, an instance of expectation–maximization.

What it changed

HMMs ran speech recognition for about two decades, and the same recipe spread to part-of-speech tagging, gene finding and handwriting. It is the clearest example of the mixed park: people decide what exists, data decides how likely it is.

The weak point was everything still cut by hand: the inventory of stations, the allowed paths, and the features describing each slice of audio, which were engineered too. Around 2012, neural networks began replacing the hand-designed acoustic parts, and later the whole pipeline.

HMMs were the core of speech recognition for about twenty years, and were widely used for part-of-speech tagging, gene finding and handwriting recognition. They are the clearest example of the pattern of that era: hand-designed structure, learned parameters.

The hand-designed parts were the limit: the state inventory, the topology and the acoustic features. From around 2012, deep neural networks replaced the Gaussian-mixture acoustic models (hybrid DNN-HMM systems), and later end-to-end models replaced the pipeline.
