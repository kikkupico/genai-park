Before this

"Can machines think?" had no agreed meaning, so Turing replaced it with a game that could be scored. A judge exchanges typed messages with two hidden players, a person and a machine, and must say which is which. If the judge can't reliably tell, the question of thinking is, for practical purposes, answered.

"Can machines think?" had no operational definition. Turing replaced it with a test: an interrogator exchanges typed messages with a human and a machine, and must identify which is which. If the interrogator can't do better than chance, the machine passes.

In the walk

Turing's judge looks only at footprints and asks: did a person make these? It never asks where the walk ended. That standard, footprints that pass for human, became what the field trains toward.

In the mechanism

The imitation game. Its modern form is maximum-likelihood training: wear the trails so the walker's footprints match human ones, step by step. Most entries up to 2020 train this way. A closer look: a judge at every footprintcross-entropy as a per-token imitation test

Turing's judge reads a finished conversation. Modern training puts a judge at every footprint instead. Take a human sentence, hide the next word, and ask the walker how much of its blaze it gave to the word the person actually wrote. The score is the negative log of that share:

Averaged over billions of footprints, this is the loss that almost every language model in this guide is trained to lower. It is the imitation game, played one step at a time: would a person have stepped here?

Look at what the judge never checks. In the figure, "Walking was written by Emerson" passes just as easily as the true sentence, because the judge is scoring whether footprints look human, not whether the walk arrives anywhere real. A model trained this way learns that "written by" is followed by a plausible author's name. Whether it is the right name depends on whether the trails happened to be worn deep enough there.

Turing made a concrete forecast: in about fifty years, a machine with roughly 109 bits of storage would play well enough that an average judge would have no more than a 70% chance of picking correctly after five minutes of questions.

The test judges a whole transcript. Language-model training applies a version of it at every token: given a human-written prefix, how much probability did the model assign to the token the human actually wrote? The loss is −log p:

Averaged over the training corpus, this is the cross-entropy loss that nearly every model in this guide minimizes. Minimizing it is maximum-likelihood estimation: making the model's distribution match the distribution of human text.

The objective doesn't measure truth. "Walking was written by Emerson" and "Walking was written by Thoreau" are both fluent, and the loss rewards whichever continuation the training data makes more probable. A model learns that "written by" is followed by an author's name. Whether it produces the right name depends on how strongly that particular fact is represented in the data.

Turing made a forecast: in about fifty years, a machine with about 109 bits of storage would play well enough that an average interrogator would have no more than a 70% chance of identifying it correctly after five minutes.

What it changed

The paper is remembered for the game, but its last section, on learning machines, matters as much here. Turing argued that rather than programming an adult mind by hand, it would be easier to build a child machine and educate it. That is the choice between cutting paths and wearing trails, made in 1950, decades before anyone could afford the trails.

The game itself became a culture of judging by output: chatbot contests such as the Loebner Prize from 1991, benchmarks scored on answers alone, and in 2014 the GAN, where a second network plays judge for the first.

The paper's final section, "Learning Machines", proposed building a "child machine" and training it rather than programming adult behavior directly: the choice between learned and hand-written systems, made in 1950.

The test also set a habit of evaluating systems purely by their outputs. Chatbot competitions such as the Loebner Prize ran from 1991, benchmarks score only answers, and the GAN (2014) turned the interrogator into a trained discriminator.
