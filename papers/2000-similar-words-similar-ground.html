Before this

The paper names the result the curse of dimensionality: a model of 10 consecutive words from a 100,000-word vocabulary has about 1050 possible cells, and no corpus fills more than a sliver of them. An n-gram model treats each word as an atomic symbol, with no relation to any other. In the walk

The ground itself gets shape. Words are placed so that similar words lie close together. A blaze worn in after "the dog was" now carries over to "the cat was", a junction no walker passed, because the two stand on similar ground. Here the ground stops being laid out by hand and starts to grow from data.

In the mechanism

Each word becomes a learned vector, and a neural network predicts the next word from the vectors of the previous few. Similar words get similar vectors, so evidence is shared between them. word2vec (2013) made such vectors cheap to learn at scale.

\[p_\theta(x_t\mid x_{t-n+1:t-1}) = \operatorname{softmax}\big(b + Wv + U\tanh(d + Hv)\big),\qquad v = [\,C(x_{t-1}); \dots; C(x_{t-n+1})\,]\]

Each word \(w\) has a learned vector \(C(w)\in\mathbb{R}^m\). Similar words get nearby vectors, and the model is smooth in \(v\), so evidence is shared between them. word2vec (2013) made such vectors cheap to learn at scale.

A closer look: ground that is learnedlearned distributed representations

Each word gets a position: a short list of numbers, a few dozen in the original experiments, that places it on the ground. To read the blaze, the model takes the positions of the last few words, feeds them through a small neural network, and gets a probability for every word in the vocabulary. The positions and the network are learned together, by the same nudging used to wear trails.

The paper's own illustration: having seen "The cat is walking in the bedroom" should make "A dog was running in a room" likelier, because "cat" and "dog", "the" and "a", "walking" and "running", "bedroom" and "room" play similar roles. Training pushes such words to nearby positions, since they predict similar next words. Once they are close, every walk through one also wears the ground around the other.

The cost was speed. Computing a probability for every word in the vocabulary at every step was expensive in 2000. word2vec (2013) stripped the idea down to the ground alone: no deep network, just positions trained so that a word predicts its neighbors, cheap enough to run on billions of words. Its positions turned out to have direction as well as distance. The best-known example: the position of "king", minus "man", plus "woman", lands near "queen".

Each word is mapped to a learned feature vector \(C(w)\in\mathbb{R}^m\), with \(m\) a few dozen in the original experiments. To predict the next word, the model concatenates the vectors of the previous \(n-1\) words into \(v\), passes them through a \(\tanh\) hidden layer, and applies a softmax over \(V\). The table \(C\) and the network weights are trained jointly by gradient ascent on the log-likelihood:

\[\max_{\theta,\,C}\ \sum_t \log p_\theta(x_t\mid x_{t-n+1:t-1})\]

The paper's illustration: having seen "The cat is walking in the bedroom" should raise the probability of "A dog was running in a room", because the pairs cat/dog, the/a, walking/running and bedroom/room play similar roles. Words that predict similar contexts receive similar vectors, and because the model is a smooth function of those vectors, probability assigned to one sentence spreads to its neighbors.

The bottleneck was the cost of computing a softmax over the whole vocabulary at every position. word2vec (2013) dropped the hidden layer and trained only embeddings. Skip-gram, for instance, predicts each word's neighbors within a window of size \(k\):

\[\max\ \sum_t \sum_{\substack{-k\le j\le k\\ j\neq 0}} \log p(x_{t+j}\mid x_t),\qquad p(o\mid w) = \frac{\exp(u_o^\top v_w)}{\sum_{o'} \exp(u_{o'}^\top v_w)}\]

with cheap approximations to the softmax, which scaled to billions of words. The resulting vector space has linear structure: \(v_{\text{king}} - v_{\text{man}} + v_{\text{woman}}\) is close to \(v_{\text{queen}}\).

What it changed

Every neural language model since starts the same way: each token is turned into a learned vector before anything else happens. In the blaze section's terms, this is where the compass directions come from. The ground is no longer cut by hand at the word level. It is worn in from text, and what counts as similar is whatever helps predict.

Every subsequent neural language model begins by mapping tokens to learned embeddings. These are the vectors whose dot products with the model's hidden state give the next-token logits. Lexical similarity is no longer defined by hand; it is whatever geometry helps prediction.
