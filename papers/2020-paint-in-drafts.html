Before this

The one-stroke painters were fast but fragile: GANs were hard to train and narrow in what they painted, and VAEs were blurry. The 2015 paper took an idea from physics: a drop of ink spreading through water is easy to describe step by step, and each small step can in principle be run backward. If you could learn to undo one small step of spreading, you could start from fully spread-out noise and work back to a picture.

One-pass generators were fast but had problems: GANs were unstable and dropped modes, and VAEs were blurry. The 2015 paper borrowed from non-equilibrium statistical physics: a diffusion process that gradually destroys structure is easy to define, and if each small step is learned in reverse, data can be generated from noise.

Formally: fix a noising chain \(q(x_t\mid x_{t-1})\) that takes data \(x_0\) to noise \(x_T\), and learn the reverse steps \(p_\theta(x_{t-1}\mid x_t)\).

In the walk

The painter learns to walk. It starts from a canvas of pure noise and at each step paints a slightly cleaner version. A pen finishes each word before the next. A brush touches every part of the picture a little on each pass, coarse to fine. Either way, every intermediate state is a draft of the product.

In the mechanism

Training adds noise to real images bit by bit, and a network learns to undo one bit. Generation starts from pure noise and runs the undoing for many steps. The 2015 paper set out the idea; the 2020 paper made it produce high-quality images.

\[\mathcal{L}_{\text{simple}} = \mathbb{E}_{t,\,x_0,\,\varepsilon}\Big\|\,\varepsilon - \varepsilon_\theta\big(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\varepsilon,\ t\big)\Big\|^2\]

Training noises a real image to a random step \(t\) and predicts the noise \(\varepsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)\). Generation starts from \(x_T\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)\) and applies the learned reverse step \(T\) times. The 2015 paper set out the idea; the 2020 paper made it produce high-quality images.

A closer look: how a draft painter is trainedthe forward process and the denoising objective

Wearing the trails (training). Take a real picture and add a small amount of random noise, again and again, a thousand times in the 2020 paper, until nothing remains but static. The amounts are fixed in advance, so you can jump straight to any step. Then train a network on one simple task: shown a noisy picture and told which step it is, predict the noise that was added. The loss is just the squared difference between the real noise and the guess.

Walking (generation). Start from pure static. Ask the network what noise it sees, remove a little of it, and repeat, step by step back to zero. Early steps settle the composition: where the sky is, where the tree stands. Late steps add detail. Each pass is a draft, and unlike a one-stroke painter, every later step can fix what an earlier one got wrong.

The cost is many steps, one network pass each. A thousand passes per image was slow; later samplers brought it down to tens of steps, and further work to a handful.

The 2015 paper set out the method. The 2020 paper made it work well, chiefly by predicting the noise rather than the clean image and by simplifying the training loss, and showed sample quality competitive with the best GANs.

Training. A fixed forward process adds Gaussian noise over \(T\) steps (\(T = 1000\) in DDPM) with a variance schedule \(\beta_1,\dots,\beta_T\):

\[q(x_t\mid x_{t-1}) = \mathcal{N}\big(\sqrt{1-\beta_t}\,x_{t-1},\ \beta_t I\big),\qquad q(x_t\mid x_0) = \mathcal{N}\big(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0,\ (1-\bar\alpha_t) I\big)\]

where \(\alpha_t = 1-\beta_t\) and \(\bar\alpha_t = \prod_{s\le t}\alpha_s\). The closed form lets training sample \(t\) directly. A network \(\varepsilon_\theta\), a U-Net in DDPM, takes the noisy image and \(t\) and predicts the added noise; the simplified loss is \(\mathcal{L}_{\text{simple}}\) above, a mean squared error.

Sampling. Start from \(x_T\sim\mathcal{N}(0, I)\) and, for \(t = T,\dots,1\):

\[x_{t-1} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}\Big(x_t - \frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}}\,\varepsilon_\theta(x_t, t)\Big) + \sigma_t z,\qquad z\sim\mathcal{N}(0, I)\]

Early steps determine global layout; later steps add detail. Because each step conditions on the current \(x_t\), errors from earlier steps can be corrected.

The cost is one network evaluation per step, so \(T\) evaluations per sample. A thousand was slow; later samplers such as DDIM reduced this to tens of steps, and distillation methods to a few.

The 2015 paper introduced the framework. DDPM (2020) made it competitive, mainly through the noise-prediction parameterization \(\varepsilon_\theta\) and the simplified, reweighted loss, with sample quality rivaling GANs.

What it changed

Within two years, nearly every leading image system was a diffusion model: DALL·E 2, Imagen and Stable Diffusion among them, and later video and audio generators. It also split generation in two. A pen walks word by word, each final once written. A brush walks over the whole canvas many times, nothing final until the end.

A draft painter paints something plausible. On its own it has no idea what you wanted.

It contrasts with autoregressive text generation: tokens are generated once each, left to right, while a diffusion model refines the whole output over many steps.

An unconditional diffusion model samples from the data distribution, with no control over which image it generates.
