The Perceptron: A Probabilistic Model for Information Storage and Organization in the Brain (1958)

Before this

In 1948 and 1950, walkers only copied sequences of footprints word for word. Another branch of the field wanted machines that could read a set of clues—light and dark patches of a letter, say—and make a choice. In 1943 McCulloch and Pitts drew a brain cell as a tiny switch: it adds up the signals arriving on its wires and fires if the total passes a threshold. Wired the right way, such switches could compute any logic, but someone had to set every wire in advance, like planting a signpost by hand and leaving it pointing the same way forever. Hebb, in 1949, guessed that real brain pathways strengthen with traffic, like trails worn in grass. Rosenblatt, a psychologist at Cornell, asked how a machine could wear its own rule in from mistakes.

McCulloch and Pitts (1943) modelled a neuron as a threshold unit: it sums weighted binary inputs and fires if the sum exceeds a threshold. Networks of such units can compute any Boolean function, but their weights were fixed by design. Hebb (1949) proposed that synaptic connections strengthen with correlated activity. Rosenblatt asked how a network of threshold units could acquire its weights from examples.

A McCulloch–Pitts unit computes \(\hat y = \mathbb{1}\big[\sum_i w_i x_i \ge \tau\big]\) with \(w\) and \(\tau\) fixed by the designer. The perceptron keeps the unit and learns \(w\).

In the walk

Instead of a keeper planting a signpost by hand, earlier walkers wear the choice in from experience. Unlike 1948, where trails formed only between exact words someone had already walked, trails here run from clues on the ground—damp grass, a downward slope—toward the pond or the gate. Because every spot shows some mix of those clues, weighing them sorts the whole park at once, including spots no walker has stood on yet.

How the trails wear in is new too: sheer foot traffic changes nothing. Reach the right place and the grass is left alone; end up at the gate when you wanted the pond, and a guide nudges the pull of every clue showing there. It makes one choice rather than a whole walk, yet every later walker in this guide reads its blaze through rows of clearings worn in this way.

In the mechanism

A perceptron computes a weighted sum of its inputs plus a bias and outputs one class if the sum is positive, the other if not. Training is error-driven: weights change only when the output is wrong, and then they move toward the correct answer. It is the first learning rule for an artificial neuron that came with a working machine.

\[\hat y = \mathbb{1}\big[w^\top x + b > 0\big],\qquad w \leftarrow w + \eta\,(y-\hat y)\,x\]

The update is zero when \(\hat y = y\), so weights change only on mistakes, and then toward the correct label. The decision boundary \(\{x : w^\top x + b = 0\}\) is a hyperplane: in the figure, a line.

A closer look: how wrong turns wear the rulethe learning rule

Each clue wears a trail pulling toward the pond or toward the gate, wider where that clue counts for more, plus a steady lean of its own for when no clues show. Standing on a spot, the walker adds up the pull of the clues underfoot. Over zero, it heads for the pond; otherwise, for the gate. After each walk:

  • Reached the right place. Leave the trails alone.
  • Went to the gate, should have reached the pond. Widen the pull toward the pond for every clue showing here.
  • Went to the pond, should have reached the gate. Pull those clues back toward the gate.

Across the map of clues, that sum draws one straight boundary on the ground, as in the figure, and each correction tilts or shifts it. If some straight line can sort every spot, this rule is certain to find one after a limited number of mistakes, a guarantee proven a few years after the paper (Block and Novikoff, 1962).

Rosenblatt's machine had a middle row of clearings too, but the paths into them were wired at random and never changed; only the last stretch was worn in. His group later built it in hardware, the Mark I Perceptron (around 1960): a 20 × 20 grid of light sensors for clues, with electric motors turning potentiometers to store each clue's pull. It learned to tell simple shapes and letters apart. The press had run ahead of it from the start: reporting a 1958 demonstration of the rule on an IBM 704 computer, the New York Times wrote that the Navy expected the machine to "walk, talk, see, write, reproduce itself and be conscious of its existence."

Given an input \(x\in\mathbb{R}^n\), weights \(w\) and bias \(b\), the perceptron predicts

\[\hat y = \begin{cases}1 & w^\top x + b > 0\\ 0 & \text{otherwise.}\end{cases}\]

For each training example \((x, y)\), with label \(y\in\{0,1\}\):

\[w \leftarrow w + \eta\,(y-\hat y)\,x,\qquad b \leftarrow b + \eta\,(y-\hat y)\]
  • Correct output. \(y-\hat y=0\), so nothing changes.
  • False negative (\(y=1,\ \hat y=0\)). \(\eta x\) is added to \(w\).
  • False positive (\(y=0,\ \hat y=1\)). \(\eta x\) is subtracted.

The decision boundary \(w^\top x + b = 0\) is a hyperplane: a straight line in two dimensions. The perceptron convergence theorem (Block; Novikoff, 1962): with the bias absorbed into \(x\), if some unit vector \(u\) separates the classes with margin \(\gamma\) and \(\|x\|\le R\) for every example, the rule makes at most

\[\left(R/\gamma\right)^2\]

mistakes, however long it runs.

Rosenblatt's architecture had three layers: sensory units, association units with random, fixed connections, and response units whose weights were learned. Only the last layer was trained. The Mark I Perceptron (around 1960) implemented it in hardware, with a 20 × 20 photocell array as input and potentiometers driven by electric motors as weights, and learned to classify simple shapes and letters. Press coverage overstated the work from the start: reporting a 1958 demonstration simulated on an IBM 704, the New York Times wrote that the Navy expected it to "walk, talk, see, write, reproduce itself and be conscious of its existence."

What it changed

The perceptron was the first machine to wear a rule in from labeled examples instead of having a signpost planted by hand. The idea at its heart—nudge each clue's pull by how much it contributed to a wrong turn—is the seed of how every neural network in this guide is trained.

The setback came in 1969. Minsky and Papert's book Perceptrons proved what one direct layer of clue-trails can't do: it can't tell whether exactly one of two clues holds, whether a count is odd or even, or whether a shape is all in one piece. The book is often blamed for drying up neural-network funding for more than a decade, though the wider "AI winter" of the mid-1970s had many causes. Rosenblatt died in 1971.

The perceptron was the first trainable classifier with a working implementation, and error-driven weight updates remain the basis of neural network training. In modern terms it is a linear classifier, closely related to logistic regression and linear support vector machines.

Left open

Weighing clues in one step can only split the ground with a straight line, so “pond if exactly one clue holds” defeats it. Rows of clearings in between could bend the line, but nobody knew how to wear trails into the middle rows when only the end of the walk is judged.\(\hat y = \mathbb{1}[w^\top x + b > 0]\) can only represent linearly separable functions. XOR is not one: it needs \(b \le 0\), \(w_1 + b > 0\), \(w_2 + b > 0\) and \(w_1 + w_2 + b \le 0\), and adding the middle two contradicts the others. Multi-layer networks can represent XOR, but there was no method for training their hidden layers. → 1986
