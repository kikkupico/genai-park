A Mathematical Theory of Communication (1948)

Before this

Before 1948 nobody could say how much information a message carries, or how predictable a language is. Shannon wanted to measure both, and for that he needed a model of how text gets produced: a source that emits one symbol after another, each with some probability. He did not try to say what the symbols mean. He only asked how likely each one is, given the ones before it.

Before 1948 there was no quantitative definition of information, and no way to say how predictable a language is. Shannon modelled a message source as a stochastic process that emits one symbol at a time, each with a probability conditioned on earlier symbols. Meaning was deliberately out of scope. Only the statistics mattered.

Formally, a source emits \(x_1, x_2, \dots\) with probabilities \(p(x_t \mid x_{<t})\). Shannon's approximations truncate the context to the last \(n-1\) symbols: \(p(x_t\mid x_{<t}) \approx p(x_t \mid x_{t-n+1:t-1})\).

In the walk

The first trails are counts. For each pair or triple of words, how often did people step from here to there? The walker turns round, looks at the last few footprints, and reads the blaze from the tally.

In the mechanism

Shannon generated English by choosing each word in proportion to how often it followed the previous word in real text. That is an n-gram model: the next word depends only on the last n−1.

\[p(x_t\mid x_{<t}) \approx \hat p(x_t\mid x_{t-n+1:t-1}) = \frac{c(x_{t-n+1:t})}{c(x_{t-n+1:t-1})}\]

where \(c(\cdot)\) counts occurrences in a corpus. This ratio is the maximum-likelihood estimate. Shannon sampled English from it, one word at a time.

That table is the whole model at that junction. Now stand on "cat". Only one walker ever left it, toward "sat", so "the cat ran" has no trail at all, even though "the dog ran" is well worn. The tally has no idea that cats and dogs are alike. That gap is what the 2000 entry fills.

Shannon built his examples by hand. For word pairs he opened a book at a random page, found the current word, and wrote down whatever word followed it there, then repeated from the new word. The result reads like English in short stretches and wanders off over longer ones: "the head and in frontal attack on an English writer that the character of this point is therefore another method for the letters that the time of who ever told the problem for an unexpected." Every pair in it is fine. The walk as a whole goes nowhere, because each step saw only one footprint back.

Looking further back helps, but the tally grows explosively. With a vocabulary of 10,000 words there are 100 million possible pairs and a trillion possible triples, and most of them never occur in any amount of text. Decades of work went into patching the empty cells: borrowing counts from shorter contexts when a longer one was unseen (backing off) and shaving a little probability off seen events to give to unseen ones (smoothing).

Take a training corpus of three sentences: the dog ran, the dog sat, the cat sat. The bigram estimate is \(\hat p(w\mid \text{the}) = c(\text{the}, w)\,/\,c(\text{the})\), with \(c(\text{the})=3\):

\(w\)\(c(\text{the}, w)\)\(\hat p(w\mid\text{the})\)
dog20.67
cat10.33
ran, sat, the00

This table is the whole model for that context. \(\hat p(\text{ran}\mid\text{cat})=0\), even though \(\hat p(\text{ran}\mid\text{dog})=0.5\): the model has no notion that "cat" and "dog" are similar, so nothing seen for one transfers to the other. The 2000 entry addresses this.

Shannon sampled from such models by hand. For the second-order word approximation he opened a book at a random page, found the current word, and took the word that followed it there. The output is locally fluent and globally incoherent: "the head and in frontal attack on an English writer that the character of this point is therefore another method for the letters that the time of who ever told the problem for an unexpected."

Increasing n gives more context but makes the data far sparser. With \(|V| = 10^4\) there are \(|V|^2=10^8\) possible bigrams and \(|V|^3=10^{12}\) possible trigrams, and most never occur in any corpus. Decades of work went into estimating the unseen ones: smoothing, which moves some probability mass from seen to unseen events, and back-off, which falls back to a shorter context when a longer one is unseen. The simplest smoothing, add-one, uses

\[\hat p(w\mid u) = \frac{c(u,w)+1}{c(u)+|V|}.\]

What it changed

The framing outlived the counts. A language model is still a probability for the next symbol given the ones before, and it is still scored by how surprised it is by real text, which is Shannon's entropy under another name. In a follow-up, Prediction and Entropy of Printed English (1951), he had people guess text letter by letter to estimate how predictable English is. Training a modern model asks exactly that question, with a far better counter.

Counted n-gram models were the workhorse of speech recognition and machine translation into the 2010s.

The formulation stuck. A language model is still a conditional distribution over the next token, and it is still evaluated by cross-entropy (or perplexity) on held-out text, which is Shannon's entropy measured against a model. His 1951 paper Prediction and Entropy of Printed English estimated that entropy by having people predict text letter by letter.

Smoothed n-gram models were the standard language model in speech recognition and statistical machine translation into the 2010s.
