Before this

Neural networks of the 1980s took a fixed-size input. To handle a sequence, you showed them a window of the last few items, which is the n-gram's glance back in a different form. Elman's idea was to let time show up in the network itself: copy the hidden layer's activity at each step and feed it back in at the next, so that what came before shapes how the next word is read.

Feed-forward networks of the 1980s took fixed-size inputs, so sequences were handled with a sliding window of recent items, which has the same fixed-context limit as an n-gram. Elman proposed representing time implicitly: feed the hidden layer's previous activation back as an additional input, so the network carries state from step to step.

Formally, a window gives \(p(x_{t+1}\mid x_{t-k+1:t})\). A recurrent network replaces it with \(p(x_{t+1}\mid h_t)\), where \(h_t = f(h_{t-1}, x_t)\) summarizes the entire prefix.

In the walk

The walker stops looking back. Instead, each footprint goes into a pack of fixed size that it carries. New things push old things down, and old things fade. By the end, "wildness" is a smudge.

In the mechanism

A recurrent network keeps a hidden state, updated each step from the last state and the new word. The LSTM's gates control the fading, so things last longer, but the pack stays fixed-size.A recurrent network keeps a hidden state, updated each step from the previous state and the new token. \[h_t = \tanh(W x_t + U h_{t-1} + b),\qquad p_\theta(x_{t+1}\mid x_{1:t}) = \operatorname{softmax}(V h_t)\]

The same \(W, U, V\) are used at every step, and \(h_t\) has a fixed dimension \(d\) however long the prefix. The LSTM's gates control what the state keeps and what decays.

A closer look: how the pack is packedthe recurrence and its gradients

At each step the network combines two things, the word just stepped on and the pack it is carrying, into a new pack:

packt = squash( A · wordt + B · packt−1 )

The same A and B are used at every step, so the walker uses one packing method all the way down the road. The blaze for the next word is read from the pack alone.

Elman trained small networks to predict the next word in simple made-up sentences and then looked inside the packs. Words that played similar roles had produced similar packs: nouns grouped apart from verbs, and among nouns, animals apart from objects. No one had told the network about grammar. It had to discover those groupings to predict well.

Training revealed the pack's leak. Learning that a word ten steps back mattered means passing a correction backward through ten repackings, and with each one the signal shrinks or explodes. In practice ordinary recurrent networks learned only short-range patterns. The LSTM (1997) added a separate compartment, the cell, whose contents pass from step to step mostly unchanged, plus learned gates that decide what goes in and what comes out. A forget gate, which decides what to throw away, was added in 1999. The pack still has a fixed size, but things put in it can last.

At each step the hidden state is updated from the current input and the previous state:

\[h_t = \tanh(W x_t + U h_{t-1} + b)\]

The same \(W\) and \(U\) are applied at every position, and the next-token distribution is computed from \(h_t\) alone.

Elman trained small networks on next-word prediction over sentences from a simple artificial grammar, then clustered their hidden states. Words with similar syntactic and semantic roles had similar representations: nouns separate from verbs, animate nouns separate from inanimate. The categories were never labeled; they emerged from the prediction objective.

Training uses backpropagation through time. The gradient reaching a token \(k\) steps back passes through \(k\) Jacobians,

\[\frac{\partial h_t}{\partial h_{t-k}} = \prod_{s=t-k+1}^{t} \operatorname{diag}\!\big(1 - h_s^{2}\big)\,U,\]

so it tends to vanish or explode geometrically in \(k\), and plain RNNs learn mainly short-range dependencies. The LSTM (1997) adds a memory cell with an additive update, gated by learned sigmoids \(i_t, f_t, o_t\) computed from \(x_t\) and \(h_{t-1}\):

\[c_t = f_t \odot c_{t-1} + i_t \odot \tilde c_t,\qquad h_t = o_t \odot \tanh(c_t)\]

Because \(c_t\) is updated by addition, \(\partial c_t/\partial c_{t-1} = f_t\), and gradients can survive many steps. The 1997 cell had no forget gate (effectively \(f_t = 1\)); \(f_t\) was added in 1999. The state is still a fixed-size vector, but it can now retain information over many steps.

What it changed

From about 2014 to 2017, LSTMs were the standard tool for anything sequential: speech recognition, handwriting, translation, early text generation. The 2014 entry is built on them.

They also made the pack's two costs plain. Everything known about the road must squeeze into one fixed-size state, and training can't be split up: step 5 can't be computed until step 4 is done. The transformer was built to remove both.

From about 2014 to 2017, LSTMs were the default architecture for sequence tasks: speech recognition, handwriting recognition, machine translation and language modelling. The 2014 entry is built on them.

They also made two costs explicit. All information about the past has to be compressed into one fixed-size vector, and computation is sequential in time: step t can't be computed before step t−1, which limits parallelism during training. The transformer removes both.

Left open

The pack is small and leaky, and training must replay every walk in order, because step 5 needs the pack from step 4.
