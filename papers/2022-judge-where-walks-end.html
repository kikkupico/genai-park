In the walk

A new source of trails. Instead of copying footprints, the walker takes whole walks, and a judge marks how each one ended. The trails are re-worn toward walks that ended well. A leash keeps them near the old trails, so the walker doesn't wander off the ground it knows.

In the mechanism

People compare pairs of outputs, and a reward model learns their preferences. The language model is then tuned with reinforcement learning to score higher, with a penalty (the KL term) for drifting from the pretrained model. A closer look: three steps to a new trail sourcethe InstructGPT pipeline

The 2017 paper showed the core idea on simulated robots and Atari games, where a reward is hard to write down. People watched pairs of short clips and picked the better one; a model learned to predict their choices and became the reward. With about an hour of human comparisons, a simulated robot learned to do a backflip.

InstructGPT applied it to language in three steps:

  • Show. People write good responses to prompts, and the model is fine-tuned on them: ordinary footprint-copying, on better footprints.
  • Judge. The model writes several responses to each prompt; people rank them. A reward model learns to predict the rankings. This is the judge at the end of the walk, now a network that can score walks no person has seen.
  • Re-wear. The model writes responses, the reward model scores them, and reinforcement learning shifts the trails toward high scores. A penalty grows as the trails move away from the step-one model: the leash.

The leash matters because the judge is imperfect. Left free, a walker finds walks the reward model scores highly that people wouldn't: longer, more flattering, more confident. This is called reward hacking.

The headline result: people preferred answers from a 1.3-billion-parameter InstructGPT over those from the 175-billion-parameter GPT-3. A small model with the new trails beat a model over 100 times its size without them.

The 2017 paper introduced the core method for tasks where a reward function is hard to specify, in simulated robotics and Atari. Humans compared pairs of short trajectory clips, a reward model was fit to their preferences, and the agent was trained with RL against it. A simulated robot learned a backflip from about an hour of human feedback.

InstructGPT applied it to language models in three stages:

  • Supervised fine-tuning. Labelers write demonstrations for prompts, and the pretrained model is fine-tuned on them.
  • Reward modelling. The model samples several outputs per prompt, labelers rank them, and a reward model is trained on the pairwise comparisons (a Bradley–Terry loss).
  • RL fine-tuning. The policy is optimized with PPO to maximize the reward model's score minus β · KL(policy ‖ SFT model).

The KL term matters because the reward model is an imperfect proxy. Without it, the policy exploits the reward model's errors, producing outputs it scores highly but humans don't prefer, such as longer, more sycophantic or overconfident text. This is reward hacking.

The headline result: labelers preferred outputs of the 1.3B-parameter InstructGPT model to those of the 175B-parameter GPT-3, a model over 100 times larger.

What it changed

ChatGPT, released at the end of 2022, was trained this way, and the method, or close variants of it, became standard for assistants. Later work such as Direct Preference Optimization (2023) learns from the same comparisons without a separate reward model or reinforcement-learning loop.

In the park, it is the first time trails are worn by where walks end rather than by copying footprints. The judge still only sees finished walks, though. While walking, the walker is as blind to the road ahead as ever.
