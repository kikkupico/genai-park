Learning Representations by Back-Propagating Errors (1986)

Before this

After Perceptrons, neural networks were a fringe pursuit for more than a decade. The cure was known in principle: put rows of posts between the clues and the last signpost, and together they can split the ground with bent lines, not only straight ones. The problem was teaching them. When the last post points wrong, which of the middle posts was at fault, and by how much? This is the credit-assignment problem. The mathematics for answering it had been worked out before, by Linnainmaa in 1970 and Werbos in 1974, among others. Rumelhart, Hinton and Williams showed it working, and showed what the middle posts learned.

After Perceptrons, neural network research was marginal for more than a decade. Multi-layer networks were known to be more expressive, but there was no practical way to assign credit to hidden units for the network's errors. Reverse-mode differentiation, the core of the solution, had been described earlier, by Linnainmaa (1970) and, applied to neural networks, by Werbos (1974), among others. This paper demonstrated it convincingly and showed that the hidden units learn useful internal representations.

Formally: for a loss \(\mathcal{L}\) and every weight \(w_{ij}\), including those into hidden units, compute \(\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial w_{ij}\), efficiently and all at once.

In the walk

Now the signposts stand in rows, and every one can learn. The first row reads the ground; each later row reads the row before it; the last points the walker. When a walk ends wrong, the blame travels back from the last post to the first, and each post turns its dials by its share.

No walker ever sees the middle posts, and nobody tells them what to look for. Yet they end up reading clues of their own. That is the ground, learned instead of cut. Every walker from here on, with its pack, its word ground or its dog, is built from such rows, and trained by passing the blame back.

In the mechanism

A multi-layer network of units with smooth activations. The forward pass computes the output; the error against the target is measured; the backward pass applies the chain rule layer by layer to get the gradient of the error with respect to every weight; and each weight takes a small step against its gradient. The backward pass costs about as much as the forward pass, however many weights there are.

\[\delta_j = \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial z_j} = \sigma'(z_j)\sum_k w_{jk}\,\delta_k,\qquad \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial w_{ij}} = a_i\,\delta_j\]

One forward pass computes every activation \(a_j\); one backward pass computes every \(\delta_j\), from the output layer down; then \(w \leftarrow w - \eta\,\nabla_w \mathcal{L}\). The backward pass costs about as much as the forward pass, however many weights there are.

A closer look: how the blame is sharedthe backward pass

The blame moves back one row at a time:

  • The last post's blame is how far off it was.
  • A middle post's blame is the blame of each post it feeds, times how much that post heeded it, all added up. A post nobody heeds gets no blame.
  • Each dial turns in proportion to its post's blame and to how strongly its clue was showing.

For this to work the posts need soft edges. A perceptron's signpost snaps from one way to the other, which gives no hint of which way to turn a dial. The paper's posts lean gradually instead, so a small turn always makes a small difference.

The paper's showcase was two family trees of twelve people each, one English and one Italian. The network was given facts such as "Colin's father is James", and asked to name the missing person. A row of six hidden posts saw each person. Nobody told them about nationality, generation or which side of the family someone was on, yet individual posts came to stand for exactly those. Trained on most of the facts, the network got most of the held-out ones right.

Each unit \(j\) computes \(z_j = \sum_i w_{ij}\,a_i\) and outputs \(a_j = \sigma(z_j)\), so \(\sigma'(z_j) = a_j(1-a_j)\). For squared error against targets \(y_o\), and \(\delta_j = \partial\mathcal{L}/\partial z_j\):

\[\begin{aligned}\mathcal{L} &= \tfrac12\textstyle\sum_o (a_o - y_o)^2\\ \delta_o &= (a_o - y_o)\,\sigma'(z_o) &&\text{output units}\\ \delta_j &= \sigma'(z_j)\textstyle\sum_k w_{jk}\,\delta_k &&\text{hidden units}\\ \Delta w_{ij}(t) &= -\eta\,\delta_j\,a_i + \alpha\,\Delta w_{ij}(t-1) &&\text{update, with momentum } \alpha\end{aligned}\]
  • Credit assignment. A hidden unit's error signal is the sum of the error signals of the units it feeds, weighted by its outgoing weights.
  • Differentiability. The perceptron's step function \(\mathbb{1}[z>0]\) has zero gradient almost everywhere. The sigmoid makes every unit differentiable, so gradients flow.
  • Momentum. The \(\alpha\) term adds a fraction of the previous weight change to each update, to speed descent.

The showcase was a family-tree task: two isomorphic trees of twelve people, English and Italian, encoded as triples such as (Colin, father, James). The network had a six-unit hidden layer encoding the input person. After training, individual hidden units encoded nationality, generation and branch of the family, features never present in the local input encoding. The network generalized to most held-out triples.

What it changed

Passing the blame back became how neural networks are trained, and it still is: every network in the rest of this guide learns this way, including through time for the pack in 1990. In 1989 LeCun's group used it to train a network that read handwritten zip codes, with posts sharing their dials across the picture: an early convolutional ground for pictures, and a direct ancestor of 2012.

But in the 1990s networks lost ground again, to methods with tidier mathematics and guaranteed answers, such as support vector machines. Deep stacks of posts were simply too hard to train.

Backpropagation became the standard training algorithm for neural networks and remains so; automatic differentiation in modern frameworks is its generalization. Backpropagation through time trains the recurrent networks of 1990. LeCun et al. (1989) trained a convolutional network with backpropagation to read handwritten zip codes.

Through the 1990s, kernel methods such as support vector machines, with convex training objectives, overtook neural networks on many benchmarks. Deep networks in particular were difficult to train.

Left open

Deep stacks barely learned: passed back through many rows, the blame faded to almost nothing. And there were too few walks and too little power to wear in deep ground. The pack's gates, in 1997, were built partly against that fading.Each layer multiplies the error signal by \(\sigma'(z)\,w\), and \(\sigma' \le \tfrac14\), so through many layers it shrinks geometrically, or explodes if the weights are large (Hochreiter, 1991; Bengio et al., 1994). Data and compute were also too limited to train deep networks. LSTM (1997) was designed partly to counter vanishing gradients through time. → 2012
