Trails

The Walker's Dog

Why does a transformer take the whole input again for every word it writes? Because its walker remembers nothing. It keeps a dog instead.

Each picture below is a small animation. The first three set up the walk; the rest answer one question across seventy years of language models: what does the walker look at before each step?

01 · the walk

The walk is the product

In the walk

The walker starts at the gate and writes one word per footprint. Nothing is handed over at the end except the footprints themselves.

In the mechanism

Autoregressive generation. The model emits one token and appends it. That growing sequence is both the input to the next step and the final output.

02 · the blaze

Each step, a blaze

In the walk

Where the road ends, the ground ahead is marked. Some ways are wide, some faint. The walker picks one and steps onto it.

In the mechanism

At each position the model outputs a probability for every word in its vocabulary. Decoding reads it: take the top one, or sample. A real blaze has tens of thousands of hairline branches; this one shows three.

03 · trails

Trails are worn in by training

In the walk

Before our walker arrives, thousands of others have crossed the park. Where they went, the grass wore down. The blaze is how worn each way is, seen from this spot.

In the mechanism

Training on next-word prediction. For every position in every training text, the weights are nudged to make the actual next word likelier. The wear generalises: the walker can read it on roads nobody walked exactly.

The question

What does the walker look at before each step?

The blaze depends on where the walker stands, and "where" means the road so far. Each family of models answers differently how much of that road the walker gets to see. Three answers follow.

04 · the n-gram walker

Glance back: the n-gram walker

A Mathematical Theory of Communication (1948) → A Neural Probabilistic Language Model (2003)

In the walk

The walker turns round and looks at just the last few footprints. Everything before them might as well not exist.

In the mechanism

The Markov assumption: the next word depends only on the previous n−1. Shannon's word approximations and classic n-gram counts work this way. The 2003 neural model replaced counts with learned word vectors but kept the fixed window.

05 · the recurrent walker

Carry a pack: the recurrent walker

Finding Structure in Time (1990) → Long Short-Term Memory (1997)

In the walk

The walker never looks back. Each footprint goes into a pack of fixed size. New things push old things down, and old things fade. By the end, "wildness" is a smudge.

In the mechanism

A hidden state, updated each step from the last state and the new word. The LSTM's gates control the fading, so things last longer, but the pack stays fixed-size. And training must walk in order: step 5 needs the pack from step 4.

06 · the transformer walker

Send the dog: the transformer walker

Attention Is All You Need (2017)

In the walk

The walker carries nothing. Before every step it sends its dog back down the whole road. The dog sniffs every footprint and runs back, and only then does the walker step. One footprint later, the dog goes again.

In the mechanism

No state passes between steps. The prediction at each position is computed from all the tokens before it, through self-attention. That is why the whole input is needed for every token: the prefix is the only memory there is.

Why give up the pack? Training speed. With no pack, every footprint of a training text can send its dog at the same moment, so a whole text trains in parallel. A pack forces the walk to be replayed in order. It also removes the bottleneck: the road keeps every word exactly, and the dog decides at each step what matters now.

Two rules for the dog

  • It forgets. Each run starts fresh. A dog that remembered earlier runs would be a pack again.
  • It runs only back, never ahead of the walker. That is the causal mask. Running ahead to try out futures is scouting, which is a different thing.

07 · query, key, value

The nose: what the dog brings back

Scaled dot-product attention, from Attention Is All You Need (2017)

In the walk

The dog doesn't fetch one footprint. It sniffs all of them, and to its nose some smell much stronger than others. It brings back a blend, mostly of what smelled strongest. This nose is trained for "what is the walk about?", so "preservation" and "wildness" come back strong.

In the mechanism

Each earlier token offers a key (its scent) and a value (what can be fetched). The current position has a query (the scent this dog hunts for). Query against key gives a match, a softmax turns matches into weights, and the dog returns the weighted sum of values. The nose is learned weights: terrain, worn in by training.

08 · heads and layers

A pack of dogs, in relays

Multi-head attention and stacked layers, from Attention Is All You Need (2017)

In the walk

One nose isn't enough. The walker keeps several dogs, each trained to a different scent, and they run together. Their findings are written onto the footprints. A second round then sniffs the annotated road. Only after the last round does the blaze open.

In the mechanism

Several dogs per round is multi-head attention; rounds are layers. Each layer updates every position's representation, and the next layer attends over the updated ones. Each footprint's notes were written by its own dogs, looking only further back. GPT-3 (Language Models are Few-Shot Learners, 2020) runs 96 rounds of 96 dogs.

09 · the KV cache

Scent marks: a faster run, not a shorter one

In the walk

Each time the dog works a footprint, it leaves a mark. Next step, it reads the marks at a run and stops properly only at the newest footprint. The run is faster, but it still covers the whole road.

In the mechanism

Keys and values for earlier tokens are stored. Because of the causal mask, an earlier token's keys and values never change when later tokens arrive, so they are computed once. The cache removes recomputation, not reading: attention per step still grows with length. Chat APIs take the whole conversation each call for the same reason; prompt caching keeps the marks between calls.
