GenAI Park

Where understanding generative AI is a walk in the park. Literally.

Every generative model, from Shannon's word counts to today's chatbots and image painters, can be drawn as one picture: a walker in a park, making something one step at a time. This guide traces how that walker changed over seventy-five years, one idea at a time.

The park

Six words for the whole field

Three short animations set up the picture. The other three arrive where history needs them.

the walk

The walk is the product

In the walk

The walker starts at the gate and writes one word per footprint. Nothing is handed over at the end except the footprints themselves. A step once taken stays taken. The park has many gates: a walk can begin with any first word, or wherever someone hands over a stretch of road.

In the mechanism

Generation one token at a time. Each token is appended to the sequence, and that growing sequence is both the input to the next step and the final output.

the blaze

Each step, a blaze

In the walk

Where the road ends, the ground ahead is marked. Some ways are well trodden, some barely marked. The walker sees only these marks and the road behind it. There is no map of the park.

In the mechanism

At each step the model outputs a probability for every possible next token. Decoding reads it: take the top one, or sample. A real blaze has tens of thousands of hairline branches; this one shows three.

In the walk

The blaze isn't printed on the ground. Two walkers can reach the same footprint, "the", by different roads, and each arrives facing a different way. The trodden ways fan out widest in the direction the walker faces, so the same spot gives a different blaze.

In the mechanism

At each step the model sums up the road so far as one long vector: a direction. Every word has a direction too, and a word's likelihood follows how closely it lines up with the one the walker faces (a dot product, then a softmax). The real compass has thousands of dimensions; the picture uses two.

trails

Trails are worn in by training

In the walk

Before our walker arrives, many others have crossed the park. Where they went, the grass wore down. The blaze is how worn each way is, seen from this spot. The rest of the story is mostly about where the trails come from, and what the walker can see when it reads them.

In the mechanism

Training. For every position in every training example, the model is nudged to make what actually came next a little likelier. Who the earlier walkers are, people, judges or the model itself, changes over the decades.

Cut paths and worn trails. A cut path is laid by hand: crisp edges, its width a designer's decision. A worn trail is made by walkers: soft edges, and the likelier a way, the more trodden it is. Early systems cut their paths; modern ones wear them; for decades, many did both.

The timeline

Seventy-five years of walkers

Each entry is one idea, named by its paper. The picture shows what that idea changed in the park; the box at the end names the problem it left for the next one.

1948 · Where the trails come from

Count the footprints, glance back

A Mathematical Theory of Communication (1948)

In the walk

The first trails are counts. For each pair or triple of words, how often did people step from here to there? The walker turns round, looks at the last few footprints, and reads the blaze from the tally.

In the mechanism

Shannon generated English by choosing each word in proportion to how often it followed the previous word in real text. That is an n-gram model: the next word depends only on the last n−1.

Left open

The walker sees only a few footprints back. And at a junction nobody has walked, there are no counts and so no blaze: a tally can't tell that "cat" is like "dog". → 2000

1950 · Where the trails come from

Judge the footprints

Computing Machinery and Intelligence (1950)

In the walk

Turing's judge looks only at footprints and asks: did a person make these? It never asks where the walk ended. That standard, footprints that pass for human, became what the field trains toward.

In the mechanism

The imitation game. Its modern form is maximum-likelihood training: wear the trails so the walker's footprints match human ones, step by step. Most entries up to 2020 train this way. The GAN (2013 entry) made the judge literal: a second network that judges footprints and nothing else.

Left open

Footprints can look human and lead nowhere. That is what a hallucination is. Judging where walks end has to wait. → 2022

1966 · Where the trails come from

Cut the paths by hand

ELIZA: A Computer Program for the Study of Natural Language Communication Between Man and Machine (1966)

In the walk

A different kind of park: a formal garden. Every path was cut by hand, and a signpost at each junction names the pattern it answers. Nothing is worn; nobody counted anything. The walker matches what it was handed against the signs and follows the path the matching sign points to.

In the mechanism

ELIZA's script was a list of keywords and decomposition patterns, each with reassembly rules that reused parts of the input. "I am X" could become "How long have you been X?" No probabilities, no data: rules written by hand. The same approach ran through decades of symbolic AI.

Left open

Every path has to be cut by someone. Step off the cut paths and there is nothing at all. Real language is far too big a park to lay out by hand. → 1989

1989 · The ground

A garden, worn in

A Tutorial on Hidden Markov Models and Selected Applications in Speech Recognition (1989)

In the walk

The two kinds of path meet. Designers still lay out the garden, deciding which stations exist and which paths join them. But they no longer decide how likely each path is. Walkers wear that in: here, thousands of recorded voices saying "speech".

In the mechanism

A hidden Markov model is a generative model: it describes how a sequence of sounds is produced by walking through hidden states. Designers chose the states and allowed transitions; the probabilities were learned from data with the Baum–Welch algorithm. This hand-laid structure with learned numbers was how statistical AI worked until deep learning, around 2012.

Left open

Worn trails can only form on paths someone cut. The ground itself, which states exist and what counts as similar, is still laid out by hand. → 2000

1990 · What the walker sees

Carry a pack

Finding Structure in Time (1990) → Long Short-Term Memory (1997)

In the walk

The walker stops looking back. Instead, each footprint goes into a pack of fixed size that it carries. New things push old things down, and old things fade. By the end, "wildness" is a smudge.

In the mechanism

A recurrent network keeps a hidden state, updated each step from the last state and the new word. The LSTM's gates control the fading, so things last longer, but the pack stays fixed-size.

Left open

The pack is small and leaky, and training must replay every walk in order, because step 5 needs the pack from step 4. That makes training slow to scale. → 2014

2000 · The ground

Similar words, similar ground

A Neural Probabilistic Language Model (2000) → Efficient Estimation of Word Representations in Vector Space (2013)

In the walk

The ground itself gets shape. Words are placed so that similar words lie close together. A blaze worn in after "the dog was" now carries over to "the cat was", a junction no walker passed, because the two stand on similar ground. Here the ground stops being laid out by hand and starts to grow from data.

In the mechanism

Each word becomes a learned vector, and a neural network predicts the next word from the vectors of the previous few. Similar words get similar vectors, so evidence is shared between them. word2vec (2013) made such vectors cheap to learn at scale.

Left open

Better ground, but the walker still glances back only a few footprints. Seeing further stays with the pack, and later the dog. → 2014

2013 · Pen and brush

Paint in one stroke

Auto-Encoding Variational Bayes (2013) · Generative Adversarial Nets (2014)

In the walk

Images need a different walker: a painter, whose product is the canvas rather than the footprints. These first painters make the whole picture in a single stroke from a random seed. It is hardly a walk at all, just one step.

In the mechanism

Both turn a random vector into an image in one pass of a network. The VAE learns this alongside an encoder that maps images back to seeds. The GAN learns it against a critic that tries to tell painted pictures from real ones: Turing's judge, turned into a trainer.

Left open

One stroke can't correct itself. VAE pictures came out blurry; GANs were unstable to train and prone to painting the same few pictures. The painter needed drafts. → 2020

2014 · What the walker sees

A dog on the other road

Sequence to Sequence Learning with Neural Networks (2014) → Neural Machine Translation by Jointly Learning to Align and Translate (2014)

In the walk

Translating means two roads: the source sentence, and the walk being written. At first the whole source was squeezed into one pack before the walk began. Then the walker got a dog. Before each step, the dog runs along the source road, sniffs every word, and comes back with a blend, mostly of what smelled strongest.

In the mechanism

Sequence-to-sequence models encode the source into one fixed vector and decode from it. Attention lets the decoder, at every step, take a fresh weighted mix of all the source positions. The weights are recomputed each step; the lines in the picture are those weights.

Left open

The dog only works the source road. On its own road the walker still carries a pack, and training still walks in order. → 2017

2017 · What the walker sees

Drop the pack, keep the dog

Attention Is All You Need (2017)

In the walk

The walker carries nothing at all. Before every step it sends its dog back down its own road. The dog sniffs every footprint and runs back, and only then does the walker step. One footprint later, the dog goes again. This is why these models take the whole input again for every word: the road is the only memory there is.

In the mechanism

No state passes between steps. Each new token is computed from all the tokens before it through self-attention. A transformer runs many dogs at once, each with its own nose (heads), in several relay rounds (layers). It also caches each footprint's scent so later runs go faster, but every run still covers the whole road.

Why give up the pack? Training speed. With no pack, every footprint of a training text can send its dog at the same moment, so a whole text trains in parallel, and the field could finally train on enormous amounts of text. Two rules keep it honest: the dog forgets after each run (otherwise it would be a pack again), and it runs only back, never ahead of the walker.

Left open

The walker can now see its whole road, and it can be trained on almost anything written. What happens when it is? → 2018

2018–2020 · Handed to the walker

Wear the whole park, then hand over the road

Improving Language Understanding by Generative Pre-Training (2018) → Language Models are Unsupervised Multitask Learners (2019) → Language Models are Few-Shot Learners (2020)

In the walk

Wear trails across most of what people have written, and the walker can continue nearly any road. So a task becomes road: someone lays the first stretch, an instruction or a few examples, and the walker finishes it. The prompt is not a command; it is road behind the walker.

In the mechanism

Pretraining a transformer to predict the next token on a vast corpus. GPT-2 showed that tasks can be posed as text to continue. GPT-3 showed that a few examples in the prompt work without any retraining, which is called in-context learning.

Left open

The walker continues roads the way people did, helpful or not, true or not. It has been trained on footprints, not on whether a walk ended well. → 2022

2020 · Pen and brush

Paint in drafts

Deep Unsupervised Learning using Nonequilibrium Thermodynamics (2015) → Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models (2020)

In the walk

The painter learns to walk. It starts from a canvas of pure noise and at each step paints a slightly cleaner version. A pen finishes each word before the next. A brush touches every part of the picture a little on each pass, coarse to fine. Either way, every intermediate state is a draft of the product.

In the mechanism

Training adds noise to real images bit by bit, and a network learns to undo one bit. Generation starts from pure noise and runs the undoing for many steps. The 2015 paper set out the idea; the 2020 paper made it produce high-quality images.

Left open

The painter paints a plausible picture. But which picture? → 2021

2021 · Handed to the walker

Name the destination

Classifier-Free Diffusion Guidance (2021)

In the walk

A text-to-image painter is handed a destination: a card saying what the picture should be. The card is never painted. The painter keeps it beside the canvas and glances at it before every stroke. Guidance sets how hard it looks: closer to the card, at the cost of variety.

In the mechanism

One network is trained both with and without the text prompt. At each denoising step the two predictions are compared, and the difference is amplified by the guidance scale. This replaced an earlier method that needed a separate image classifier.

Left open

Road or card, the walker only gets what it's handed at the start. Nobody has yet told it how its walks turned out. → 2022

2022 · Where the trails come from

Judge where walks end

Deep Reinforcement Learning from Human Preferences (2017) → Training language models to follow instructions with human feedback (2022)

In the walk

A new source of trails. Instead of copying footprints, the walker takes whole walks, and a judge marks how each one ended. The trails are re-worn toward walks that ended well. A leash keeps them near the old trails, so the walker doesn't wander off the ground it knows.

In the mechanism

People compare pairs of outputs, and a reward model learns their preferences. The language model is then tuned with reinforcement learning to score higher, with a penalty (the KL term) for drifting from the pretrained model. The 2017 paper introduced learning a reward from human comparisons, for simulated robots and games; InstructGPT applied the method, leash included, to following instructions.

Left open

The judge rewards how walks end, but the walker still can't look ahead before it steps, and a wrong step midway stays. → 2022

2022 · Looking ahead

Scout before answering

Chain-of-Thought Prompting Elicits Reasoning in Large Language Models (2022) → Tree of Thoughts: Deliberate Problem Solving with Large Language Models (2023) → DeepSeek-R1: Incentivizing Reasoning Capability in LLMs via Reinforcement Learning (2025)

In the walk

The walker can't see ahead, so it walks ahead on scratch paper: footprints that work the problem out before the answer. Once the answer is written, those footprints stop being the product. Scouting means walks taken only to choose the real one. Tree of Thoughts scouts several branches and drops the bad ones. Reasoning models are trained, by judging only the final answer, to scout long and well.

In the mechanism

Prompting, and later training, a model to write intermediate reasoning before its answer improves accuracy on multi-step problems. Tree of Thoughts runs a search over partial reasoning. DeepSeek-R1 trained long reasoning with reinforcement learning, rewarding only checkable final answers.

Left open

A walker can't take back a footprint: "backing up" means walking on down a road that says "that was wrong". And no amount of scouting finds a fact the walker never saw. → 2023

2023 · Handed to the walker

Let other hands lay road

Retrieval-Augmented Generation for Knowledge-Intensive NLP Tasks (2020) → Toolformer: Language Models Can Teach Themselves to Use Tools (2023)

In the walk

Midway, the walker can call for help: a search, a calculator, a database. Another hand lays a stretch of road with the result, and the walker carries on from the new end. It still has no map. What it gets is more road, not a view of the park. A chat works the same way: each of your messages is road laid by another hand.

In the mechanism

Retrieval-augmented generation fetches relevant passages and adds them to the model's input. Toolformer trained a model to write API calls into its own text, with the results spliced in. Either way the model reads the whole road again, including the new stretch.

Left open

As more of the world's writing is walked by walkers, the next walkers' trails get worn by their footprints. → 2023

2023 · Where the trails come from

Trails worn by walkers' own footprints

The Curse of Recursion: Training on Generated Data Makes Models Forget (2023)

In the walk

Train each generation of walkers on footprints left by the one before, and the trails narrow. The common way gets wider; rare ways fade until nobody walks them. Self-play game players escape this, because a win or a loss keeps correcting their trails. Here nothing does.

In the mechanism

Training repeatedly on model-generated data loses the tails of the original distribution first, then drifts toward a narrow version of it. The authors call this model collapse. Keeping some original human data in the mix slows it down.

Left open

Still open: where will the next trails come from?

The story so far

Two motions across the decades

Where the trails come from has moved steadily away from human hands. These overlap in time; the order is how much human-made road each one needs.
