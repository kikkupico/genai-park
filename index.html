GenAI Park

Where understanding generative AI is a walk in the park. Literally.

Every generative model, from Shannon's word counts to today's chatbots and image painters, can be drawn as one picture: a walker in a park, making something one step at a time. This guide traces how that walker changed over seventy-five years, one idea at a time.Every generative model, from Shannon's n-gram counts to today's chat assistants and image generators, produces its output one step at a time, sampling each step from learned probabilities: \(x_t \sim p_\theta(\cdot \mid x_{<t})\). This guide traces how those models changed over seventy-five years, one idea and one landmark paper at a time.

the walkgeneration

The walk is the productAutoregressive generation

In the walk

The walker starts at the gate and writes one word per footprint. Nothing is handed over at the end except the footprints themselves. A step once taken stays taken. The park has many gates: a walk can begin with any first word, or wherever someone hands over a stretch of road.

In the mechanism

Generation one token at a time. Each token is appended to the sequence, and that growing sequence is both the input to the next step and the final output.

A model defines a distribution over whole sequences by the chain rule:

\[p_\theta(x_{1:T})=\prod_{t=1}^{T} p_\theta(x_t \mid x_{<t})\]

Generation samples \(x_t \sim p_\theta(\cdot\mid x_{<t})\), appends it, and repeats until an end token. The growing sequence is both the next input and the output.

the blazethe distribution

Each step, a blazeEach step, a probability distribution

In the walk

Where the road ends, the ground ahead is marked. Some ways are well trodden, some barely marked. The walker sees only these marks and the road behind it. There is no map of the park.

In the mechanism

At each step the model outputs a probability for every possible next token. Decoding reads it: take the top one, or sample. A real blaze has tens of thousands of hairline branches; this one shows three.At each step the model outputs a probability for every possible next token. Decoding chooses from it: take the most likely token (greedy) or sample. A real vocabulary has tens of thousands of tokens; the figure shows three.

At each step the model outputs a distribution over the vocabulary:

\[p_\theta(v\mid x_{<t}) \ge 0,\qquad \sum_{v\in V} p_\theta(v\mid x_{<t}) = 1\]

Greedy decoding takes \(\arg\max_v p_\theta(v\mid x_{<t})\); sampling draws from it, often reshaped by a temperature \(\tau\), as \(p \propto \exp(\ell_v/\tau)\) for logits \(\ell_v\). Real vocabularies have \(|V|\) in the tens of thousands; the figure shows three.

In the walk

The blaze isn't printed on the ground. Two walkers can reach the same footprint, "the", by different roads, and each arrives facing a different way. The trodden ways fan out widest in the direction the walker faces, so the same spot gives a different blaze.

In the mechanism

At each step the model sums up the road so far as one long vector: a direction. Every word has a direction too, and a word's likelihood follows how closely it lines up with the one the walker faces (a dot product, then a softmax). The real compass has thousands of dimensions; the picture uses two.At each step the model summarizes the context as a hidden-state vector. Every token has an output embedding, and a token's logit is the dot product of the two; a softmax turns logits into probabilities. Real models use thousands of dimensions; the figure uses two.

The context is summarized as a vector, and each token \(v\) has an output embedding \(e_v\):

\[h_t = f_\theta(x_{1:t}) \in \mathbb{R}^d,\qquad p_\theta(x_{t+1}=v\mid x_{1:t}) = \frac{\exp(e_v^\top h_t)}{\sum_{u\in V}\exp(e_u^\top h_t)}\]

Two contexts ending in the same token have different \(h_t\), so different distributions. Real models use \(d\) in the thousands; the figure uses \(d=2\).

trailstraining

Trails are worn in by trainingProbabilities are learned in training

In the walk

Before our walker arrives, many others have crossed the park. Where they went, the grass wore down. The blaze is how worn each way is, seen from this spot. The rest of the story is mostly about where the trails come from, and what the walker can see when it reads them.

In the mechanism

Training. For every position in every training example, the model is nudged to make what actually came next a little likelier. Who the earlier walkers are, people, judges or the model itself, changes over the decades.Training. For every position in every training example, gradient descent, with gradients from backpropagation, adjusts the parameters to raise the probability of the token that actually came next. Where the training data and signal come from, human text, human judgments or the model's own outputs, changes over the decades.

Training minimizes the average negative log-likelihood of the data, with gradients from backpropagation:

\[\mathcal{L}(\theta) = -\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{t}\log p_\theta\big(x^{(n)}_t \mid x^{(n)}_{<t}\big),\qquad \theta \leftarrow \theta - \eta\,\nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}(\theta)\]

Continuations that are frequent in the data get high probability. What the data \(x^{(n)}\) and the training signal are, human text, human judgments or the model's own outputs, changes over the decades.

Cut paths and worn trails. A cut path is laid by hand: crisp edges, its width a designer's decision. A worn trail is made by walkers: soft edges, and the likelier a way, the more trodden it is. Early systems cut their paths; modern ones wear them; for decades, many did both.

Hand-designed versus learned. In a hand-designed system, people specify the rules or structure directly. In a learned system, parameters are fit to data. Early systems were designed; modern ones are learned; for decades, many did both: hand-designed structure with learned probabilities.
