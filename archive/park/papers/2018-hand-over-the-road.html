Before this

Until 2018, each language task usually got its own model trained on its own labeled examples: one for sentiment, one for question answering, one for entailment. Pretrained word vectors were shared, but little else. The question was whether one walker, trained on plain text with no labels, could carry most of the skill.

Until 2018, most NLP tasks used separate models trained on task-specific labeled data. Pretrained word embeddings were shared, but little else was. The open question was whether unsupervised pretraining of a whole model on raw text could transfer to many tasks.

Formally: pretrain one model on \(\mathcal{L}(\theta) = -\sum_t \log p_\theta(x_t\mid x_{<t})\) over raw text, then pose each task as a prefix \(c\) and read the answer as a continuation \(y\sim p_\theta(\cdot\mid c)\).

In the walk

Wear trails across most of what people have written, and the walker can continue nearly any road. So a task becomes road: someone lays the first stretch, an instruction or a few examples, and the walker finishes it. The prompt is not a command; it is road behind the walker.

In the mechanism

Pretraining a transformer to predict the next token on a vast corpus. GPT-2 showed that tasks can be posed as text to continue. GPT-3 showed that a few examples in the prompt work without any retraining, which is called in-context learning.

\[\text{pretrain: }\ \min_\theta\ -\sum_t \log p_\theta(x_t\mid x_{<t}),\qquad \text{use: }\ y\sim p_\theta(\cdot\mid c)\]

Fine-tuning (GPT) updates \(\theta\) on labeled data. Zero-shot and few-shot prompting (GPT-2, GPT-3) leave \(\theta\) fixed and put the task description and examples into \(c\). Adaptation moves from the parameters to the context.

A closer look: three papers, three ways of handing overfine-tuning, zero-shot, few-shot

2018: wear first, then adjust. The first paper pretrained a transformer to predict the next word over about 7,000 unpublished books, then fine-tuned it briefly on each task. One pretrained model, lightly adjusted, beat models built for each task on most of the benchmarks tested.

2019: no adjusting. The second scaled up to 1.5 billion parameters, trained on about 40 GB of web pages linked from Reddit, and tried tasks with no fine-tuning at all, just by laying the right road. Append "TL;DR:" after an article and the walker writes a summary, because on the web that is what usually follows.

2020: a few examples. The third, at 175 billion parameters, showed that a few worked examples in the road are enough. The paper's own illustration:

Translate English to French:
sea otter => loutre de mer
cheese =>

The walker continues with "fromage". Its trails were not changed at all. It is reading the pattern from road it was handed. This is called in-context learning, and it got markedly better as models grew.

GPT (2018). A transformer decoder pretrained with the language-modelling objective \(L_1\) on BooksCorpus, about 7,000 unpublished books, then fine-tuned on a labeled dataset with a small task head, keeping language modelling as an auxiliary loss:

\[L_3 = L_2 + \lambda L_1,\qquad L_2 = -\sum_{(x,y)} \log p_\theta(y\mid x)\]

It improved on task-specific architectures on most of the benchmarks evaluated.

GPT-2 (2019). 1.5 billion parameters, trained on WebText, about 40 GB of web pages linked from Reddit. Evaluated zero-shot, with no fine-tuning: since a task is a conditional \(p(\text{output}\mid\text{input},\text{task})\), the task can be specified in the input text itself. For example, appending "TL;DR:" to an article induces a summary.

GPT-3 (2020). 175 billion parameters. Given a task description and \(K\) demonstrations in the context window, it performs the task with no gradient updates:

Translate English to French:
sea otter => loutre de mer
cheese =>

The model outputs "fromage". This is in-context learning: \(\theta\) is fixed, and the task is inferred from \(c\). Few-shot performance improved substantially with model size.

What it changed

The prompt became the interface. Instead of training a model per task, people write road for one model, which is the habit everything from chat assistants to agents relies on. It also shifted where the skill lives: mostly in pretraining, worn in from enough text that nearly any road has been walked before in some form.

Its limit is built in. A walker trained only to continue text continues it the way the text did: helpfully or not, truthfully or not.

Prompting became the main interface to language models: one set of weights, many tasks, specified in text. Most capability now comes from pretraining, and adaptation happens through the context rather than through parameters.

The objective sets the limit. A model trained only to continue text reproduces the distribution of its training data, which includes unhelpful, false and harmful text.

Left open

The walker continues roads the way people did, helpful or not, true or not. It has been trained on footprints, not on whether a walk ended well.Pretraining fits \(p_\theta\) to the training distribution, \(\min_\theta \mathrm{KL}(p_{\text{data}}\,\|\,p_\theta)\).
