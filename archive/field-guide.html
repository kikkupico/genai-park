Trails Field Guide

What eight landmark results each brought to the table, told through one picture: a walker on a road, choosing its next step.

Each entry draws the same picture twice: the field just before the result, and just after. The one thing the result changed is marked NEW. Then it is explained twice: as a walk, and as the mechanism in the paper's own terms. The frame behind the picture is on the Trails canvas.

The picture

What the marks mean

Road. The steps the walker has taken. The road is the product.
Blaze. The choices for the next step. Width is probability.
Trails. Where past walks went and ended well, worn in by training.
Terrain. What the walker can tell apart, and what counts as nearby.
Scouts. Walks taken only to choose the real step, then thrown away.
Given road. Laid by someone else before the walk: a prompt.

1948 · row 1 of the test

Shannon's n-grams

Claude Shannon, “A Mathematical Theory of Communication”

Changes the trailsM1 · a new source of trails
In the walk
With nothing counted, every branch is equally wide and the walker writes noise. Shannon counted which letters follow which in real English. Those counts are the first trails: after “t h”, the blaze now leans hard toward “e”. The walker's terrain is only the last few letters, so it can tell apart nothing older.
In the mechanism
Estimate the probability of the next symbol given the previous n−1 symbols by counting in a body of text. Shannon used this to build successively closer approximations of English, first with letters and then with words.
Answered
The origin. Text treated as a chain of local choices.
1988 · row 10 of the test

Temporal-difference learning

Richard Sutton, “Learning to Predict by the Methods of Temporal Differences”

Changes the trailsM1 · a new source of trails
In the walk
Trails record where walks ended well. Before TD, a junction could only learn its worth by waiting until the walk reached its end and looking back, or by knowing the whole map in advance. TD lets each junction learn from the next junction's guess. Trails wear in during the walk, one step at a time.
In the mechanism
Move the predicted value of a state toward the reward plus the predicted value of the next state, instead of toward the final outcome. Learning happens at every step and needs no model of the environment.
Answered
Bellman's equation (row 2) needs a model of every next junction, and waiting for the end of each walk is slow.
2003 · row 20 of the test

The neural language model

Yoshua Bengio, Réjean Ducharme, Pascal Vincent, Christian Jauvin, “A Neural Probabilistic Language Model”

Changes the terrainM2 · reshape the terrain
In the walk
N-gram trails exist only where some walker has already stood. At a new junction the walker has no blaze at all. Bengio gave the walker a terrain in which junctions with similar words lie close together. A trail worn at “the dog sat on” now lends its blaze to “the cat sat on”, which nobody walked.
In the mechanism
Map each word to a learned vector and predict the next word with a neural network over the vectors of the previous words. Contexts made of similar words get similar predictions, so probability spreads to sequences never seen in training.
Answered
N-gram sparsity, exposed by Shannon's counting (row 1): most long contexts never occur in any training text.
2011 · row 24 of the test

DAgger

Stéphane Ross, Geoffrey Gordon, J. Andrew Bagnell, “A Reduction of Imitation Learning and Structured Prediction to No-Regret Online Learning”

Changes the trailsM1 · a new source of trails
In the walk
A walker trained only on the expert's road has trails only on that road. One small slip puts it on ground with no trails, its next step is a guess, and the guesses compound. DAgger lets the walker walk, asks the expert what to do at the junctions the walker actually reached, and wears trails there. Recovery becomes a trail like any other.
In the mechanism
Run the current policy, have the expert label the states it visits, add those labelled states to the training set, retrain, and repeat. The training data comes to match the states the learner itself produces.
Answered
Compounding error in behavior cloning (Ross and Bagnell, 2010). In language models the same failure is called exposure bias.
2014 and 2017 · rows 31 and 36 of the test

Attention and the Transformer

Dzmitry Bahdanau, Kyunghyun Cho, Yoshua Bengio, “Neural Machine Translation by Jointly Learning to Align and Translate”; Ashish Vaswani and colleagues, “Attention Is All You Need”

Changes the terrainM2 · reshape the terrain
In the walk
A recurrent walker carries the road behind it in one fixed-size pack, and the oldest steps fade from it. Attention lets the blaze reach back along threads to any step of the road and weigh each one. To choose between “is” and “are”, it looks straight back at “keys”. The Transformer builds the walker's whole terrain out of these threads.
In the mechanism
At each step, score every earlier position, turn the scores into weights, and take the weighted sum of their representations. Bahdanau's model attended from the output to the input sentence. The Transformer drops recurrence and attends over the sequence's own past as well.
Answered
One fixed vector for the whole input in sequence-to-sequence models (row 30), and the slow, fading memory of recurrent networks (row 17).
2016 · row 35 of the test

AlphaGo

David Silver and colleagues, “Mastering the Game of Go with Deep Neural Networks and Tree Search”

Changes the scoutingM5 · scout
In the walk
Before each move, a Go program sends scouts ahead. Earlier programs sent random scouts that spread everywhere and played every game to its end. AlphaGo's scouts follow the blaze, so they stay on promising branches, and they stop early because a learned value judges a position without playing it out. Its trails came first from human games, then from games against itself.
In the mechanism
Monte Carlo tree search guided by a policy network, which proposes moves, and a value network, which scores positions. Both were trained first on expert games and then improved by self-play reinforcement learning.
Answered
Monte Carlo tree search alone (row 22) was too weak for Go: random rollouts are poor scouts on a board that large.
2020 · row 43 of the test

Diffusion

Jonathan Ho, Ajay Jain, Pieter Abbeel, “Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models”

Changes the walkM4 · change the blaze
In the walk
A one-stroke painter makes the whole picture at once and gets no chance to fix it. GANs trained such painters through a contest with a judge, and the contest often broke down. Diffusion makes the painter walk: many small strokes from noise to picture, composition first and detail last. The strokes are thrown away and the canvas is kept, and every canvas along the way is a draft of the product. This is the brush, where a language model is the pen.
In the mechanism
Train a network to remove a little Gaussian noise at each of many noise levels. Generate by starting from pure noise and denoising step by step, a thousand steps in the original paper.
Answered
Unstable adversarial training in GANs (row 29).
2022 · row 45 of the test

RLHF and InstructGPT

Long Ouyang and colleagues, “Training Language Models to Follow Instructions with Human Feedback”, building on Paul Christiano and colleagues, 2017

Changes the trailsM1 · a new source of trails
In the walk
A pretrained walker's trails are the web's routes. Asked a question, it may carry on with more questions, because that is a route web pages often take. RLHF puts a judge at the destinations: people compare finished routes, and the trails are reshaped toward the routes they preferred. A leash keeps the new trails close to the old ones, so the walker does not leave the ground it knows.
In the mechanism
Fine-tune a pretrained model on demonstrations, train a reward model on human rankings of its outputs, then optimize the model against that reward with PPO, plus a KL penalty that keeps it near the original model.
Answered
Pretrained trails follow the web, not the request (GPT-2, row 40). Rewards for “a good answer” are too hard to write by hand (row 38).
