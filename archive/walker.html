Thoreau's Walker

A real walker, trained on one essay: Henry David Thoreau's Walking (1862), 66,724 letters. Every fork below is computed live from letter counts in that text. Nothing is scripted.

Each scene rebuilds the walker with one landmark result from the field and shows the broken version beside the working one. The frame behind it is on the Trails canvas.

How to read a walk

The drawing

Road. The letters the walker has written. This is the product.
Blaze. The choices for the next letter. Width is probability. Click a branch to force it.
Stubs. Branches the walker did not take.
Worn. How often Thoreau's text passed this junction, on a log scale. A small number below means the walker remembered fewer letters there.
Given road. Laid by someone else before the walk began: the prompt.
Your steps. Letters you forced, by clicking a branch or typing.
1948 · row 1 · Shannon's n-grams

Counted trails

Shannon built approximations of English by counting which letter follows which. This walker does the same with Thoreau. The dial sets how many letters back it can tell apart, which is its terrain. The counts are its trails.

What to notice
  • With no memory the fan is flat and the road is noise. That is Shannon's zero-order approximation.
  • Each letter of memory sharpens the fans. Around three or four, words appear that Thoreau never wrote.
  • At six, most fans have a single branch, and the walker retraces Thoreau word for word. Watch the copied stretch grow. A trail worn by one walker is just one footpath.
Rows 20, 24, 33 · exposure bias

Off the trail

Both walkers remember five letters. The road starts with a word Thoreau never used, so the first junction is one no walker has passed. Type letters or click a thin branch to push them further off.

What to notice
  • The strict walker has no blaze at all. The walker that backs off guesses from fewer letters: the small numbers under its worn bars drop, then climb back as it finds familiar ground.
  • Backing off treats two junctions as alike when their last few letters match. That is terrain laid out by hand. Bengio's neural language model (2003, row 20) learned it instead.
  • This walker forgets a wrong step after five letters, because it only remembers five. A model that reads the whole road carries a wrong step forever, which is why its errors compound (rows 24 and 33).
2019 · row 41 · nucleus sampling

Reading the blaze

Same trails, same terrain, same seed. Only the rule for reading the blaze differs.

What to notice
  • Greedy falls into a loop within a few words and never leaves it.
  • At temperature 1 the sampler does fine here, because every thin branch of a counted trail is still real Thoreau. Raise it toward 2 and it wanders into made-up words.
  • Nucleus sampling cuts the thin tail, shown in grey. That mattered most for neural models, whose tails are full of guesses rather than counts. Holtzman and colleagues named the failure degeneration in 2019.
Rows 5, 22, 46 · scouting

Scouting

Before each step, the scouting walker sends six scouts eight letters ahead. It keeps the first step of the scout whose route was most worn and throws the rest away. Only that one step reaches the road.

What to notice
  • The scouts' letters never reach the road. They are scaffolding, which is the canvas's criterion at work.
  • Compare the average branch taken: the scouting walker keeps to more worn routes, and pays for it in scouted letters.
  • Chain of thought moves this scouting inside the walk itself (row 46).
